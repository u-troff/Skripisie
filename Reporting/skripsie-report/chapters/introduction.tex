\mysection{Background}{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Having a robot that takes spoken instructions and removing the two things that make most mobile robots hard to operate day to day: a screen to navigate, and an operator trained on its command set. 
For a small indoor rover this matters even more, since the tasks it can reasonably be asked to do
(check whether a door is open, look at what's on a shelf, go and see who's in the next room) are
exactly the kind of thing a person would rather say out loud than type into an app.

Until recently, building this meant hand-coding a fixed vocabulary of recognized phrases, each
mapped to a specific behavior. Large language models change that. Given a natural-language
command and a description of what the robot can do, an LLM can decompose an instruction into a
sequence of primitive actions without the vocabulary being enumerated in advance. Hori et al.\
demonstrate this for long-horizon tasks specifically, using a dialogue-based process where the
model asks questions to fill in what a one-shot instruction leaves out before committing to a
plan \cite{hori2025longhorizon}.

Two problems follow directly from handing planning to a language model instead of a fixed rule
set. First, spoken commands are often genuinely ambiguous. ``Look at the plant'' is only
well-defined if there is one plant in the room, and a system that always acts on its first guess
will act on the wrong guess often enough to matter. Ren et al.\ frame this formally: an
LLM-based planner should recognise when it does not have enough information and ask for help
rather than proceeding, and they give a taxonomy of the kinds of ambiguity (attribute, numeric,
spatial) a household or office command tends to carry \cite{ren2023knowno}. Second, language
models hallucinate. They produce fluent, confident output that does not correspond to anything
real, and Liang et al.\ show this gets worse, not better, as a task is broken into more steps,
which is a direct problem for a robot expected to carry out multi-step instructions rather than
single commands \cite{liang2024introspective}.

Both of these are usually studied against cloud-hosted, large-parameter models running on
hardware that also has more sensing than a low-cost mobile robot typically carries. The rover
used in this project for demonstration is the, a Hiwonder TurboPi. It does not fit that picture of large memory and compute power: a Raspberry Pi 5, a
mecanum chassis, a camera on a 2-DOF pan/tilt gimbal, and no wheel encoders, IMU, or LiDAR. There
is no odometry and no heading, and the mecanum wheels slip enough that an open-loop ``drive
forward two metres'' cannot be trusted. Nothing on the Pi itself can run a language or vision
model at a usable speed, and a stable internet connection cannot be assumed for a robot meant to
operate on ordinary household or office connectivity. The pipeline built has to hold up
under both constraints at once: ambiguity and hallucination have to be managed using models small
enough to run locally on commodity hardware and not the large cloud-hosted models most of the
literature above assumed.

The same idea, that a language model can reason about likely failure, also applies once a plan is
already executing, not only while it is being made. Sinha et al.\ and Elhafsi et al.\ both use an
LLM as a runtime monitor, watching a robot's observations for signs that something has gone wrong
and triggering a safety response, for a quadrotor and for autonomous-driving and manipulation
policies respectively \cite{sinha2024aesop, elhafsi2023semantic}. This project reuses that same
principle for a rover reading its camera feed mid-mission, to decide whether a plan needs
revising rather than only checking it once before departure.

\mysection{Problem statement}{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

It is not established whether a fully local language-and-vision pipeline, with no dependency on
a cloud API and small enough to run on a single consumer-grade machine, can reliably turn an
ambiguous spoken command into a plan that a low-cost, sensor-poor indoor rover can actually carry
out, or how much command complexity such a pipeline can absorb before it fails.

The literature reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature} addresses parts of this without closing
the gap. Ren et al.\ and Liang et al.\ both address ambiguity and hallucination in LLM-based
planning, but with large, cloud-hosted models and, in Ren et al.'s case, largely in simulated
tabletop manipulation rather than on a physically constrained mobile platform
\cite{ren2023knowno, liang2024introspective}. Hori et al.\ target long-horizon plans specifically
but do not measure how many chained instructions a small, locally hosted model can carry before
its output becomes unreliable \cite{hori2025longhorizon}. Sinha et al.\ and Elhafsi et al.\
monitor for failure once a task is already underway, but neither closes the loop back into a
voice-driven clarification-and-confirmation step that gates a physical robot before it moves at
all \cite{sinha2024aesop, elhafsi2023semantic}.

This project addresses that gap directly, through two research questions. The first is whether
locally hosted models, running entirely on a laptop-class machine paired with a rover that has no
odometry or IMU, can generate plans that are both correct enough given a vague command and
executable given the rover's actual capabilities. The second is how many commands can be chained
together before the pipeline starts hallucinating: at what point does compositional depth outrun
what a small, local model can reliably plan for.


\mysection{Research questions}{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

This project is assessed against two research questions, both concerned with what a fully
local speech-and-vision pipeline can and cannot do on hardware with no odometry or heading.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Can locally hosted models generate plans that a rover can actually
        execute and follow, even when the spoken command that produced them is vague?
        Answering this requires the plan to be correct enough despite an under specified
        command, and executable given what the rover can physically do.
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How many commands can be chained together before the pipeline starts
        to hallucinate? This asks how far compositional depth can be pushed before the
        model's output stops corresponding to anything the room, the command, or the
        rover's capabilities actually support.
\end{enumerate}

\mysection{Objectives}{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The main objective of this project is to build and evaluate a fully local pipeline that turns
a spoken, potentially ambiguous command into a plan an indoor rover can carry out, using models
small enough to run on a single consumer-grade machine rather than a cloud API. To answer RQ1,
this requires a multi-turn clarification loop that resolves ambiguity through spoken dialogue
before a plan is generated, a planner constrained to a capability vocabulary the rover can
actually perform, and a voice-only confirmation step that gates execution. To answer RQ2, the
pipeline must log enough about each run, the number of chained instructions, the number of
clarification turns used, and whether the resulting plan referenced anything never seen or
said, to measure how compositional depth relates to hallucination.

A secondary objective is to demonstrate plan executability. Comparing the local pipeline against equivalent cloud-hosted models is a further, secondary objective, included as a benchmark rather than as evidence for either research
question.

\mysection{Scope and limitations}{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope-and-limitations}

This project covers the local speech-to-execution pipeline: transcription, ambiguity
resolution through spoken dialogue, plan generation and verification, and a voice-gated
confirmation step. Navigation is treated as instrumentation rather than a research problem in
its own right.

The room the rover operates in is scanned once beforehand by a walkthrough video, producing a
digest the planner can ground its plans in. Extending that scan into a general-purpose mapping
system is not attempted. Cloud-hosted models appear only as a benchmarking comparison and are
not part of the system under test.

The main limitation follows from timing. The rover itself has no wheel encoders, IMU, or LiDAR, so open-loop movement commands cannot be relied on for precision, a hardware limitation this project works around rather than solves. Evaluation
is limited to a small number of test rooms and command sets, so the results characterise the
pipeline's behaviour on the cases tested rather than guaranteeing performance on arbitrary
rooms or commands.

\mysection{Report outline}{Report Outline}
\label{sec:report-outline}

Chapter~\ref{chap:literature} reviews existing work on LLM-based task planning, ambiguity
resolution, and hallucination in robotic systems, and identifies where this project's local,
resource-constrained setting differs from it. Chapter~3 describes the system design: the
speech-to-plan pipeline and its clarification loop. Chapter~4 covers the rover platform and
the interface between the planning software and the hardware it controls. Chapter~5 describes
the test methodology, including the simulated executor used in place of physical hardware.
Chapters~6 and 7 present and discuss the results for RQ1 and RQ2, and Chapter~8 concludes with
a summary and recommendations for future work.
